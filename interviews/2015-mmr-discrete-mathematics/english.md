## Alexander Vityaz: “All Roads Lead to Discrete Mathematics”

> Remarkably, there is room for humor in the interface of the Privat24 home page. Remarkable only at first glance: once you learn that the [sarcastic messages and images](https://www.dropbox.com/s/u1k826m7dbotvec/Screenshot%202015-07-09%2014.51.08.png?dl=0) are the work of Alexander Vityaz, Deputy Chairman of PrivatBank’s Management Board and Head of the E-Business Center, all questions fall away. The MMR editorial team decided to find out what prompted him to take this approach and where he finds subjects for inspiration. It is worth noting that in business and IT circles, Vityaz’s name is uttered with a certain reverential awe. And no wonder: he is largely responsible for the fact that when people say “a technology-driven bank,” many of them mean Privat. Alexander and his team are now moving to the next level, creating the operating universe of Corezoid.com.

We encounter the results of Vityaz’s work every hour, but what can we say about the man himself? Asked about his hobbies, he says he has no particular one: “Books, films, wine, beer, StarCraft, food, and so on—the order may vary.”

Vityaz was impressed by Eugene Koonin’s book *The Logic of Chance*, while the article he says turned his life upside down was “Obtaining Images of the Most Complex Mathematical Objects with a Computer,” published in *Scientific American*.

As you can imagine, MMR could not allow itself to ask banal questions, with one exception: **if it were not for programming, what would Vityaz be doing?**

> If I weren’t programming, I might have become a chemist—cooking meth; at school, I had an incredible interest in chemistry! But the computer won, and today chemistry and physics are rapidly becoming branches of finite automata theory. However long the rope may twist, all roads lead to discrete mathematics, so is it worth being distracted by special cases?

**Alexander Vityaz**

Deputy Chairman of PrivatBank’s Management Board and Head of the E-Business Center

So we abandoned the standard interview format and asked Alexander to choose, from two options, the one he personally considered more difficult. True to his reputation as a nonconformist, he expanded the boundaries of what “difficult” means.

### Which Is More Difficult?

**Developing Privat’s electronic system or seducing Angelina Jolie?**

Seducing Jolie is more difficult—she’s a little old; I’d have to force myself. In development, the most difficult part is not the development itself, but convincing people and implementing it.

**Developing something new around the clock or doing monotonous work from nine to six?**

I don’t use the word “work” for what I do. For me, things have worked out almost exactly as they did for the physicist Artsimovich: “Science is the best way to satisfy one’s personal curiosity at the state’s expense.”

**Coming up with dark jokes for the Privat24 page or running a marathon?**

We don’t come up with the jokes. There is so much material all around us that we cannot laugh at it and post it fast enough. I’ve never run a marathon; I prefer swimming and walking the dog. It is enjoyable, which means it is not difficult.

**Being head of a bank’s E-Business Center in 2015 or being a programmer in the 1990s?**

Being a programmer in the 1990s. There was almost no internet then, and access to a computer was harder to get than access to female classmates. But, to be honest, today we are materializing what I dreamed about then and sketched on scraps of paper—with adjustments for technological progress, of course. In general, the really difficult time was 2000. As Lennon put it: “Before Elvis, there was nothing.”

**Finding time for solitude or finding time for family?**

In fact, there is plenty of free time. The myth that executives are busy was invented by the “executives” themselves to lend a sacred meaning to their existence. There is time for everything that interests you.

**Turning Ukraine into a developed country or making contact with the reptilians of Nibiru?**

I think people need to be developed, rather than the country. More specifically, I see a great many IT specialists without mathematical or engineering backgrounds. They make up 90 percent, and that is a colossal problem. Everyone complains that there is a shortage of programmers—it is a myth: there are plenty of coders—a synonym for “secretary-typist”—but engineer-mathematicians are almost impossible to find. And no one has explained how to make contact with reptilians better than Longyear did in the novella/film *Enemy Mine*. So far, I haven’t managed to find a better way myself.

**Defeating global warming or defeating laziness in a single person?**

Laziness is not about Humans; it is about non-humans. Which means there is no problem (smiles). And Humans will, of course, cope with global warming—what choice do we have?

**Mastering a new programming language or reading *Fifty Shades of Grey*?**

Mastering a language is not a problem; the problem is learning to think in it. It is a way of thinking, not grammar. I did not even try to read *Fifty Shades of Grey*. I started watching the film about five times and could not get through it; apparently, I am not part of the target audience.

**Finding a worthwhile startup to use at the E-Business Center or finding a 30-year-old virgin?**

I’m not deliberately looking for startups yet. What I see on the market is a complete horror show, caused by an abundance of coders/usability specialists/activists and a lack of engineers from the real economy. There are plenty of unsolved problems everywhere, but you can only see them by immersing yourself in the relevant domain. And judging by the number of dissatisfied faces, finding a virgin of such advanced age is not all that difficult. But let’s ask ourselves honestly: why the hell would we want that kind of extreme sport?!

**For an individual to take a small step or for all humanity to take a giant one?**

The smallest unit of humanity is… a human being. “Small” steps are always taken by specific people, while humanity itself simply moves. An individual choice is always more difficult than a collective one, because you cannot pass responsibility for it to “humanity.”

**Another Ukrainian bank outdoing Privat in IT or waiting for a happy ending to *Game of Thrones*?**

Today, no bank in the world can catch up with, outdo, or technologically replicate PrivatBank. There are reasons for that.

1. Our peers in the market are trying to solve an unsolvable problem: they are trying to become technology companies. But by definition, this is impossible: a bank and a technology company have business models that point in opposite directions. A bank turns inward, while a technology company shines outward to the entire world. A bank’s business is assets and liabilities; a technology company’s business is technology. Therefore, banks have no chance of becoming technology companies while continuing to remain banks. In practice, economists and marketers are trying to manage engineers, and that is impossible by definition. To become a technology company, a bank has to change almost everything: it must follow a path like NOKIA’s, from a sawmill to… no one knows what NOKIA will become tomorrow.

2. PrivatBank is already in the post-IT space: we program less and less and do more and more engineering. There were a number of subjective prerequisites for this, and they grew into objective ones. A technology stack emerged that enables us to escape the quagmire of hardcoding. As a result, the product launch/change cycle has now shrunk from months/years to hours and days. This is no joke. But all these processes took 15 years, which is why it is physically impossible to catch up with us without a 0-transition.
