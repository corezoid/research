## 0. Background and first encounter

The clock on my smartphone read 2:20 p.m. The weather widget showed minus 8 degrees Celsius; the medical thermometer, plus 38.4. Despite three layers of clothing and a thickly wrapped scarf, the chills cut right through me.

I was riding in an icebound minibus and cursing my inability to reschedule. We had booked the meeting with Alexander Vityaz for December 17 several weeks earlier. Then, on December 16, I came down with some vile virus—the kind of thing that happens once every five years. To make matters worse, for the first time in six months all the water in my apartment had been shut off. I could not even wash my face, let alone revive myself under a hot shower.

There was, of course, a choice. I could write: “Sorry, I’m ill and cannot get out of bed, the water is off, everything is doom and rock bottom, so let’s do this some other time.” Then I could calmly continue lying there with a fever. I did not like the alternatives, though: either make a separate trip to Dnipro or wait another few weeks. Curiosity, urgency—and, somewhere in there, laziness—won. I swallowed a fever reducer and headed to Vozdvyzhenka.

This was already my third meeting with the businessman whose inventions had spent the previous twenty years pushing forward Ukraine’s banking sector—and, let us be honest, the world’s as well.

**A little about the interviewee**

With his beloved dog and external motivator—one of the rare staged photographs, perhaps the only one, taken for an article in *Forbes Ukraine*.

Vityaz was a familiar figure to many journalists of the 2000s and early 2010s. For PrivatBank, this was a period of aggressive expansion and entrenchment in the Ukrainian market. The PrivatBank team showered the press with “a world first” releases, customers grew accustomed to electronic services, and competitors hurriedly developed equivalents in an effort to keep up with the Dnipro innovators.

The man responsible for much of that race readily spoke with journalists over a mug of beer after official events. Although he was not the institution’s most public face, many people remember him from those years for his engineer’s enthusiasm and his distinctively ironic attitude toward the press.

That continued until the end of 2016. Then came nationalization, the court battles around which [continue to this day](https://finance.liga.net/bank/novosti/sud-arestoval-imuschestvo-kompaniy-kolomoyskogo-po-delu-privatbanka). Against that backdrop, [questions arose](https://biz.liga.net/all/it/article/kod-v-meshke-kak-yadro-sistemy-privatbanka-okazalos-v-amerike) about who owned PrivatBank’s famous digital services, Privat24 in particular. For several days, the issue kept the country in suspense. Once the i’s had been dotted, Alexander Vityaz left the bank and virtually disappeared from the Ukrainian media. He was succeeded by his former colleague Sergey Kharytych.

I missed all those twists, turns, and crossroads because I entered journalism later. I first crossed paths with PrivatBank’s former head of the Center for Electronic Business (CEB) in Odesa in October 2018—unexpectedly, at an event held by PUMB, the First Ukrainian International Bank, which, as it turned out, had long used Corezoid to deploy its digital solutions.

There was no shortage of adventure. We met on the eve of the main conference. Vityaz greeted us politely, ordered tea, and immediately criticized the questions that my colleague [Sergey Shevchuk](https://www.liga.net/author/sergey-shevchuk) and I had sent in advance. He did not walk away from the conversation, though. Perhaps because the tea was already on its way.

His one condition was that I not record anything: we would have an off-the-record background conversation, he said, and then meet again. I nodded but did not switch off the recorder. My plan was very simple: if the conversation was mediocre, I would delete it; if it was interesting, I would turn it into an interview—but would certainly clear the text with him. Everything was plainly heading toward the second outcome.

Alexander Vityaz and Sergey Danilenko at a PUMB conference.

I will spare you the details of Alexander Vityaz’s reaction to the text I sent him. He reacted badly. Harshly, though without swearing. Sergey Danilenko, Middleware’s marketing director and communicator, did much to defuse the situation. I apologized too, of course, belatedly realizing that this journalistic sprint had been unnecessary.

We managed to resume the conversation only several weeks later. The second time, we met in Google Docs. I poured in questions; Vityaz wrote answers. He wrote at length and in detail, often returning to the same points and honing the wording again and again. Sometimes he simply kept polishing the joke.

Eventually, the correspondence exhausted itself, and we scheduled another face-to-face meeting.

Needless to say, everyone from whom I managed to hear or read even a passing remark about Alexander Vityaz had a different opinion of him.

Partners speak of the entrepreneur respectfully, choose their words carefully, and sometimes sigh: public statements can be difficult to clear with him. Some former employees write fairly cynical posts and comments about how “Palych milked the bank.” Other former employees argue with them about it. Members of his current team say that after nationalization people clamored to speak with Vityaz—everyone wanted to be useful and “conquer the world.” Incidentally, Alexander’s team had remained almost unchanged for the previous two years.

Some say innovation at Privat ended when he left. Others say the opposite: that people gained more freedom and ideas began to flow more freely. Journalists variously describe Vityaz as funny, difficult, brilliant, or impossible.

It is easy to get lost in that heap of opinions, but one thing became clear to me: this man has left almost nobody who ever dealt with him indifferent.

Digital core. Image taken from the Corezoid website.

And so: December 17, Kyiv, Vozdvyzhenka. Alexander Vityaz, Sergey Danilenko, and I are sitting in a restaurant that smells of fish, desserts, and a hint of pretension. We had originally looked for somewhere quiet. Instead, Christmas songs entertain the diners, plates crash in the kitchen, and waiters quarrel around us—much to Vityaz’s annoyance. Amid all this din, my only hope is the recorder’s sensitivity.

In a few hours, the founder of Corezoid has a flight to Amsterdam. From there he is traveling to Canada for the holidays, where his children are studying.

“We definitely have until 5:30,” Vityaz says.

“Three hours,” I calculate, shivering with fever. “Wonderful.”

My impressions of the conversation are extraordinarily varied.

At forty-nine, the entrepreneur looks better than many younger men. He is dressed in simple jeans and a sweater. His palms are small and well cared for—clearly, his fingers have spent most of his life striking keys and turning pages rather than hauling sacks. His handshake is firm. Behind his glasses, his eyes look straight at you, studying, with a trace of mockery. Vityaz complains of insomnia: unresolved problems keep him awake at night. Yet there is no hint of fatigue in his face. His iPhone chirps periodically. The notification sound is amusingly nostalgic, like a 1990s computer or an ancient eight-bit console—a little digital *kurlyk*.

Vityaz is a master of cynical jokes. His cutting labels are far from always pleasant; his metaphors are caustic. Some of them periodically fly at Sergey Danilenko, who backs up his boss with facts and presentations. Sergey takes it calmly and with humor: he and “Alexanpalych,” as he calls him, have known each other for ten years.

At the same time, the trolling combines curiously with an old-school upbringing, making it difficult to call his manner outright rudeness. It is as though Vityaz constantly monitors a certain boundary. You notice this when he speaks harshly about what is happening in the market, then corrects himself repeatedly. The point, he says, is not to insult anyone. The point is to jolt the market awake—to make things more interesting, to create movement.

What the businessman is plainly prepared to discuss for hours is new inventions. He enthusiastically draws graphs, demonstrates the envelope-and-stamp principle behind Trigger.Cards—a technology that, in Vityaz’s view, could digitize the state at no cost—and describes the latest additions to Corezoid and how his colleagues are sharpening their neural-network skills. He also talks about distributed messengers, pipelines, and presentations to Western companies. He speaks with particular excitement about American technology giants being surprised by the ideas of a Ukrainian and his team.

Almost none of the technical material made it into the final interview. First, it is difficult to present properly to a non-engineer. Second, it deserves a separate story.

*It is approximately 8:25 p.m. Instead of the planned three hours, we have talked for six, drunk around ten small pots of tea, and outlasted several dozen diners. We are already beginning to put on our coats when Alexander Vityaz suddenly asks:*

— What do you want to get from this meeting? Is there some basic idea of what the final result should be?

**— To reveal you as a businessman, an engineer, and a person.**

— Who would find that interesting?

**— In my view, people in technology and business—and beyond. That is how we ended up doing an [interview](https://tech.liga.net/technology/interview/fizik-lourens-krauss-ne-pozvolyayte-merzavtsam-podavlyat-vas) with physicist Lawrence Krauss. It began with my personal interest and drew a major response. Besides, you have been out of public view for a long time. And you usually declare, quite categorically, “I’m not interested in talking to journalists.”**

— Why “categorically”? You are exercising your right of appeal right now. *(Laughs.)*

**— Excellent. I’ll get even on behalf of everyone.** *(I smile.)*

— Let me put it this way. A few days ago, Sergey Danilenko attended a meeting at a bank with which we had worked closely even before the crisis. Some of the same executives are still there, and they can hardly have forgotten everything, yet they treated us like a startup that had wandered in off the street. So I do not think my personality will interest anyone.

**— I disagree. And I suspect some people expect you to share the life hacks that enable you to create innovations.**

— As though I swallow a magic pill in the morning and an innovation is born? Ridiculous.

**— It doesn’t work like that? Seriously, that is precisely the myth I want to dispel: the idea that “there is no secret.” I want to show the way of thinking and the route to innovation.**

— The route is very simple. *(Laughs.)* To innovate, you need to run into a problem. To run into a problem, you need to go and work somewhere. Period.

## 1. The man and the team

**— Alexander, although you dislike boring questions, I’ll begin with the obvious one. How much did your parents influence the person you became, and in what way?**

— As I now understand it, my parents created the right conditions. After several unsuccessful attempts to influence the outcome, they stopped trying. *(Smiles.)*

Our home was always full of books and magazines. “Young Astronomer” and “Young Chemist” kits occupied every windowsill. I went to the planetarium every weekend—first with my parents, then, from the second grade onward, on independent expeditions. I would not be surprised to learn that I was the most frequent visitor in the history of the Donetsk Planetarium. From time to time, I raided neighboring apartments to rummage through their bookshelves. Some especially valuable books were not allowed out of the house, so I sat and read them at the neighbors’. They often gave me treats, which meant two pleasures at once. I had permanent access to four neighbors’ libraries, the school library, and the regional library.

Once, a book I had borrowed from a neighbor was stolen from me at school. I was terribly ashamed. My father and I went to apologize. I remember it to this day.

**— You once said that, when hiring people for a call centre, you asked candidates who Tom Sawyer was. Why?**

— Because it is a quick test, a way to establish someone’s background. I believe everyone should read that book. At ten or eleven, there is no other book as powerful.

**— What is it about for you?**

— Everything at once: friendship, love, adventure, enterprise, individualism, travel, and freedom. Today I see it as a book about freedom of choice, about the happiness of action. I am amazed it was never banned. It is about ideal capitalism. *(Smiles.)*

**— Which other books had the greatest influence on your development?**

— I think there were six such “books.” The next was probably *Spartacus*. Together with him and a dozen sets of legionaries, we fought hundreds of dramatic battles across the landscapes of our apartment. I worked through every piece of literature on the ancient world that I could find. By the fifth grade, I think I could have taught history at least as well as our teacher—and certainly more interestingly. The third “book” was collective: encyclopaedias. I always had volumes on my desk so that I could look things up quickly. I still keep several Wikipedia tabs open at all times—[this one](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%9A%D0%BE%D0%BD%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%B0_%D0%A5%D0%B0%D0%B9%D1%82%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%B0), for example.

The fourth book was thick popular-science magazines; the fifth, science fiction. The sixth was a ninth- and tenth-grade astronomy textbook, which I read in the third grade.

**— Do your reading preferences change over time?**

— Of course. They change with my mood, the context, the project. Fiction is easier to read at the airport; science and business books are easier on the plane. Right now, I’m reading Alexey Ivanov’s *Pishcheblok*.

*Alexander Vityaz hands me a tablet with an e-reader open. Fiction is mixed with books on genetics, mathematics, linguistics, consciousness, and neural networks. Judging by the percentages completed, several books are being read in parallel. My eye catches a history of AC/DC.*

— I began some of them on paper and am finishing them here. For some reason, the fact that I was reading this one—*(shows the book* *AC/DC: The Young Brothers*)—once astonished the people at Western Union. Perhaps they recruit only classical-music lovers.

**— Where did your engineering curiosity come from?**

— Probably from chemistry. In our time, it was the only applied science you could pursue more or less seriously at home. And where there is chemistry at thirteen, there is the manufacture of explosives *(Editor’s note)*; where there are explosives, there is rocket building. That means design, engines, materials, calculations, planning, sourcing chemicals, explosions, A/B testing, and so on.

But one day a programmable calculator, the first experimental computer-science lesson, and programs printed in *Tekhnika Molodezhi* defeated chemistry. *Moon Landing* on the MK-61 calculator remains the most emotional game I have ever played. At thirteen, the imagination is more powerful than any augmented reality. *(Smiles.)*

**— Is discipline one of your defining traits?**

— Responsibility is a better word. It is more universal.

**— How did you develop it?**

— I never skipped school. Not once. I was never late. I always arrived early. I am not joking.

**— Your parents trained you that way?**

— Of course. Where else would it come from at five years old? Only from your parents. So I have been highly disciplined since childhood.

**— What did you learn at school, by the way?**

— Everything they taught there: the subjects, and knowledge of how the system works. I missed alcohol and smoking at school; I mastered those later at university. What else was there to learn?

**— How to interact with classmates and teachers, for example.**

— I attended three schools. I had friends in each one, so from that point of view everything was fine. I still keep in touch with two or three school friends.

My relationships with teachers were always complicated, but I was probably fortunate: most of them were the right kind of people and were motivated. Young red-haired teachers of mathematics and chemistry, for example, definitely increased my interest in those subjects by an order of magnitude. Unfortunately, English was taught by ladies who were not quite as expressive.

**— I remember your repeated complaints about uninteresting questions and long interviews. Who, then, is an interesting conversationalist for you?**

— It varies. Sometimes you sit down with someone in a café abroad and talk for hours. The question is whether I would want to repeat that conversation ten times.

As for colleagues, university friends, and classmates, most fall away over time because everyone develops at a different speed and has different potential. Three to five people remain with whom I continue to talk.

**— More generally?**

— Intelligent and interesting people, obviously—people from fields of knowledge unfamiliar to me. Unfortunately, the most interesting conversationalists are those with whom it is physically impossible to speak. Their mystery left with them. I would be extremely interested in talking to Évariste Galois, or at least observing how it all unfolded around him: an extraordinary density of events and talent.

There is, however, a prospect of having the right conversationalists on a mass scale. We will have Chekhovs in the cloud: neural networks built from their works, letters, and other surviving texts. We will gain direct access to the greatest minds. Then you journalists will be able to conduct any interview you like, and nobody will refuse. *(Smiles.)*

**— Then it will not be only journalists. Today, now that you can speak with senior executives at megacorporations, which of them are interesting?**

— I could before, and I still can. They are good people, but overly materialistic—an occupational deformation. How else would they have become executives at megacorporations? They have regulators and boards of directors; they have neither the time nor the company in which to discuss the future. In finance—payment systems and banks—their degree of freedom is close to zero.

Entrepreneurs are interesting to speak with. Managers, more often than not, are not. They are parts of a large machine, and if the machine is well tuned, an average person can cope with the job. What is the point of talking to them?

**— You also speak with large technology companies.**

— True. But corporate gravity acts on everyone in the same way. Over time, founder-entrepreneurs are squeezed out and defeated by business managers. Today we can see that this approach no longer works: you cannot find a functioning model and exploit it unchanged for years. Corporations are being forced to find a way to restore their entrepreneurial spirit, and so far they have failed.

**In terms of talent quality, the mid-to-late 1990s were a golden age for banks. Since around 2010, that has no longer been true.**

**— You trained as an applied mathematician. Why did you take a job as a loan officer?**

— I did not choose it. There were no other suitable vacancies. In fact, there were no other decent jobs at the time. Everything I knew about how a bank worked came from Jerome, and, to my astonishment, that picture corresponded very closely to what I found. Applied mathematics is the most universal profession: it can and should be applied to anything.

**— Was Inko Bank your first job?**

— Probably my third. Before the bank, I worked evenings at a design bureau, writing programs in LISP. It structured my thinking very strongly.

**— Your biography also mentions selling computers.**

— From my third year at university, we traded floppy disks and other hardware. A fellow serviceman of mine, a student at the Moscow Institute of Oil and Gas, sent parcels or packages by train with floppy disks to Donetsk. I distributed them through fellow students and mailed the proceeds back to Moscow.

Then came modems and so on. The timing was excellent: I had been expelled from the Komsomol for chronically missing meetings and automatically lost my scholarship, so I lived quite comfortably on that money. But at the time you could not spend money without coupons, and coupons were issued only with scholarships and salaries. I was a miniature Koreiko.

**— You mean the cut-out coupon-karbovanets vouchers used to validate purchases?**

— Exactly. I found myself with a pile of money with which I could buy nothing. Friends helped me out with coupons a couple of times, but I was embarrassed to beg. I had to acquaint myself with the black market in coupons and foreign currency. In other words, I completed a practical course in banking without interrupting my studies. I was very good at it, but programming always won.

**— As a student today, would you go into banking?**

— I would not even consider it. In terms of talent quality, the mid-to-late 1990s were a golden age for banks. Since around 2010, that has no longer been true. A student should join a large company only to understand how everything works and to find real problems.

An interesting but unavailable conversationalist for Alexander Vityaz: Évariste Galois (1811–1832), the French mathematician who laid the foundations of modern higher algebra. He was also a republican revolutionary and died in a duel at the age of twenty. The density of events in his life truly is astonishing. Photo: Wikipedia.

**— Since we are talking about talent quality, how do you select people for your team?**

— There are no recipes. I take almost anyone, and then they either survive or they do not. I watch how a person works, communicates, and relates to the job. I look at everyday habits too.

**— You sound like many businesspeople: there is a result, but no recipe. Can you give us at least a hint?**

— No. It is like sex: innate. Does anyone teach people how to do it?

**— Depends on the sources.** *(Laughs.)* **They tell you to experiment and not be afraid.**

— You have to keep trying. That is the key. The function of interest puts everything in its proper place.

**— But everyone wants to understand how to experiment better and more efficiently.**

— You have to go to work every day and try something. Over time, your attempts become more accurate. That is time and experience. Here is a simple exercise: how many books do you read each month?

**— Let us say three.**

— Suppose you learn that becoming a superhuman requires reading ten books a month. How long would you last? If the impulse is not inside you, one or two months at best.

It is the same with sport. Every fitness centre is built around a property of human psychology: you buy a membership, and already you are an athlete. Eighty percent of people never turn up. That is why people who want to move every day get a dog. *(Vityaz is describing his own case here—Editor’s note.)* When you need an external motivator, you have to arrange one for yourself.

**— All right. Do you often need an external motivator?**

— For walking, yes.

**— And for working on a product or inventing an innovation?**

— Work is something you do on a schedule; “inventing” in a vacuum does not work. I do not wake up in the morning intending to invent something. You need an external irritant, a recurring problem. For me, that involves no strain at all. It is not difficult. You simply have to maintain a constant flow of information, and I begin to see patterns. Ask me how it works—I do not know.

When the incoming information stream narrows, I become bored and find a new one. Since leaving PrivatBank, I have had two serious inventions: one fundamental, the other commercially viable. The flow of day-to-day work became much smaller, so my brain occupied itself with something else simply because it cannot sit idle. That is a controlled positive effect. But in the intervals, as Alexander Valeriiovych Dubilet—PrivatBank’s chairman before nationalization—used to say, there is only melancholy. *(Smiles.)*

**— But people sometimes begin by running with a dog, then form a habit and can eventually make themselves get up without it.**

— Where did the dog go? *(Smiles.)* The issue is not making yourself get up. It is finding reasons to do so every day. The goal has to be concrete, not abstract. Understand: if you ordered me to write poetry right now and even offered me money, I would still never write it. Someone else writes whether they are paid or not. You cannot put “buy beanbags for the innovation department” in a business plan and expect an innovation to emerge six months later. It does not work that way. It works perfectly well for ticking boxes, filing reports, and making announcements.

**— How, then, were your first banking innovations born?**

— Once again, you are confusing cause and effect. Nobody ever assigned me the task of giving birth to something. Innovations are always born from problems or goals.

**— What did you encounter during development?**

— Resistance from the environment. But that is a given, not some revelation or secret. Everyone encounters friction. The question is whether you have the desire to fight it.

**You have to keep trying. That is the key. The function of interest puts everything in its proper place.**

**— When and how did you get your first direct reports?**

— They appeared when we had our first successes in retail banking at Inko. It happened organically: the people who had been helping moved over to sit with me and continued working full-time. There was no drama and no formal recruitment.

**— Did you have to retrain yourself for a new kind of communication?**

— I do not recall any fundamental difficulties. Leadership is probably largely innate. Somehow it all worked by itself. People are social animals. Most do not want to assume risk and look for a good leader whom they can trust. That is natural. Of course I read management books. My father undoubtedly influenced me greatly; he was a very Roman kind of leader. *(Smiles.)* He did not consciously teach me, but children obviously absorb a great deal from their parents.

**— How many people report to you today?**

— I do not think in terms of “subordination.” Being a slave owner is boring. I work with eighty colleagues who understand what we are building to very different degrees. *(Laughs.)*

**— All right. How readily do you bring someone into the team today? How readily do you part ways?**

— Hiring is difficult. Parting is difficult.

**— Do you look for experienced people or train them on the job?**

— We look for experience. When we cannot find it, we start growing people ourselves.

**— How involved are you in hiring?**

— Deeply. I am more interested in the result than anyone.

**— What can lead to a separation?**

— The requirements are universal and compact: Development—Responsibility—Communication. If all three indicators deteriorate, sooner or later the relationship ends.

**Breaking through ceilings is 90% teamwork.**

**— It has been two years since you left PrivatBank with part of your team. How many people have left you since then?**

— One veteran and one external hire.

**— That is quite a result. You say you are deeply involved in communicating with the team...**

— I hope I am. *(Smiles.)*

**— ...and eighty people seems like a fairly large number.**

— Not at all. I once had 1,500 people in the call centre, and I spoke with many of them. There were another 150 or so in the Center for Electronic Business. It is a function of interest, not time.

**— How inclined are your people to break through glass ceilings on their own?**

— Breaking through ceilings is 90% teamwork.

**— How do you retain people? Why do they stay with you for so long?**

— Because my colleagues are interested. As our friend, the New York banker Zindel Zelmanovich, says: “Your people are not people—they are gold.”

**— When building the team, did you pay attention to where and how people had studied?**

— At first I did. Then I realized there was no correlation. Good people reveal themselves in the work. A young man working on one of our new projects began while he was still at university, two hours a day. Many are self-taught: they arrived fairly weak, learned in battle, and are professionals today.

**— Why hire them in the first place?**

— There was nowhere to find ready-made people. And those “weak” candidates were stronger than the other weak ones. Sometimes I hired two people for a single vacancy. Whoever survived stayed.

**— So once again, the answer is simply to try.**

— What else could it be? Business is continuous A/B testing.

**— Perhaps you can see straight through people immediately, from indirect signs?**

— At most, this: sometimes someone comes in smelling bad. Sometimes they arrive in dirty shoes. But even that is a lottery—there are highly talented people among the homeless too. *(Smiles.)*

***Sergey Danilenko:*** Alexander Pavlovich once checked how many contacts candidates had in their phone books.

— I did that in the call centre, yes. How else could you tell whether a nineteen- or twenty-year-old enjoyed talking to people? The size of the phone book was a useful metric.

**— Hiring inexperienced employees is often associated with saving money at the beginning, and with training people from scratch “in your own image” so that you do not have to break habits already formed elsewhere. Does any of that apply to you?**

## 4. PrivatBank

**— It has been two years since the end of PrivatBank’s private era. What can you say about it now?**

**— I have heard your view that the state moved to nationalize the bank after it lost a large part of its business in Donbas and Crimea. Do you still believe the situation was exploited?**

Let me tell you about my own lawsuit against Privat. The proceedings are already in their second year. I am the only minority shareholder who began litigating over the shares.

The background is as follows. In 2012–2013, PrivatBank was preparing for an IPO. To make that possible, all senior executives were to receive shares. The number was calculated according to a complex formula that took years of service and achievements into account.

I arrived later than everyone else, so my service earned me 0.17 percent, or 129,291 shares. Their equivalent value was approximately $4.5–5 million. The IPO never happened: the crisis struck, and banks began trading at around one times book value. Everyone received shares and was asked to sign powers of attorney authorizing the majority shareholders, who were negotiating with potential buyers, to manage our shareholdings. Before the nationalization, an investment-banking manager came to me and said that my power of attorney had not been renewed. Since I was planning to leave, I did not want to freeze the shares for five years, as the document required. I therefore did not sign it. The other minority shareholders did.

During the nationalization, our majority shareholders signed a letter—which is available online—saying they were transferring all the bank’s shares for one hryvnia.

In reality, however, they did not transfer all the shares for one hryvnia. They transferred 100 percent minus my shares—that is, 99-point-something percent—because they had no legal authority to act on my behalf.

On that basis, I filed a lawsuit alleging that the Ministry of Finance had violated my constitutional rights. Article 41 of Ukraine’s fundamental law, the Constitution, says in plain and simple language that, in the absence of martial law, the state may requisition property by court order only after paying its full market value in advance. The Constitution clearly describes the formula under which property may be alienated. Nothing of that kind happened in the country on the day the shares disappeared from my securities registrar account.

**— That is a substantial amount. What stage has the case reached?**

**— All right. Suppose it is now almost sinful to celebrate innovation in bots. What should people celebrate?**

**— What, then, could legitimately be presented as a breakthrough at a bank?**

— You have given yourself away. Presenting something as what it is not amounts to lying and manipulation. Anything that falls under the definition of the term can be considered a breakthrough. Read Wikipedia. Why are you so desperate for everyone to be a breakthrough-maker?

*The current head of the CEB versus its former head. [More on the conflict](https://tech.liga.net/battles/article/vityaz-vs-privatbank-chem-opasen-konflikt-vokrug-privat24) between Alexander Vityaz and PrivatBank over the bank’s departure from Corezoid.*

**— Do you personally use PrivatBank?**

— I use A-Bank, monobank, and PrivatBank.

**— So you did not part with the bank as a customer. And A-Bank itself is a piece of Privat.**

— Privat is a piece of me. We built Privat—not ladies with perms.

**— In your account, there are no innovations there today. Would you radically update Privat24 and the other services?**

— That is not “my account.” It follows from the facts, the dictionary, and official statements by PrivatBank’s CEO. You can trace how we worked from 1992 onward. Extrapolate.

**— There are several views of your technological past at Privat. One says: well done—innovation, beauty. Another says all that development at cosmic speed turned into a heap of security holes, an exhausted team, and endless stress for users. A third says that, partly through your efforts, Kolomoisky seized the market and profited from it. How do you respond?**

— Are capturing the market and making money not the principal goals of any business? You must have collected opinions from Communist Party members.

**— How do you regard Kolomoisky as a businessman?**

— A strange question. He is a monument.

**— You speak rather unflatteringly about part of the team that remained at PrivatBank, while they criticize the period when you managed the CEB. Why? Did you make life miserable for all of them and leave amid conflict? Were you “authoritarian” as a manager?**

— First, my unflattering remarks concerned the new management and the practice of “alco-management.” Second, do you understand that you have just described Apple, Amazon, Tesla, and… China?

Talk about kind and cruel bosses is 100 percent infantile. Companies operating under market conditions are always authoritarian because they must make decisions quickly.

Business is a red ocean. It is a law of nature. If no one had constantly tried to eat your great-great-grandfather, you would still be eating slightly rotten fruit in a comfortable forest. Victory requires a leader, discipline, and superhuman energy. So you have placed me in the right company.

*Vityaz is alluding to Elon Musk, who is known for a fairly [hard-edged management style](https://www.businessinsider.com/tesla-employees-describe-intense-conditions-life-under-elon-musk-2018-8), although the wider public idealizes the Tesla and SpaceX founder for his business breakthroughs.*

**Armchair innovations do not exist. Innovations are born in a collision with the customer.**

**— You once said that people looking at technology today think rather linearly, including in banking. They extrapolate technologies in a straight line, whereas many factors must be considered. Can a corporate banking structure, in principle, foster technological development?**

— Who writes a book: a company or a writer? A company gives birth to nothing. People create. The question is whether the company knows how to assemble them correctly and provide the environment. But that also requires changing the decision-making system, which is practically impossible.

**— Why did it work at Privat, then?**

— Because there was one decision point, and that point made the final decision. As the leader, Alexander Dubilet legally allowed innovations to happen inside the bank. Had he forbidden it, I would have done the same work somewhere else.

**— In other words, he allowed people to create innovations inside Privat?**

— You cannot grant permission to create. If you want to do something, you will find a way to implement it. The problem is putting the innovative product on the shelf. Alexander Dubilet did that decisively in his time—something any corporation or state institution is, by definition, unable to do.

A businessperson proceeds from advantage. If intelligent, that person makes more correct decisions than incorrect ones. Their name is irrelevant. A state-owned company faces an entirely different situation. You can be three times as intelligent, but you still have to coordinate everything with everyone, all the way to the Ministry of Finance. The speed of decision-making is fundamentally different, and the company slows immediately.

**— How many times did you consider leaving PrivatBank during its development? Until what point could another organization have recruited you, and with what offer?**

— There were a couple of occasions, but there was always a reason not to leave. No one has tried to recruit me since 1999. Who would want someone like me?

The first and last person to recruit me was Dubilet. There was only one factor: I could continue doing what I was doing, but on a larger scale. So far, no one else has been prepared to attempt that. *(Laughs.)*

**— Which Ukrainian bank could emerge as a global innovator?**

— The privately owned PrivatBank emerged as a global innovator. That is a historical fact. But banks as a system have no such ambitions and cannot have them, because banking is a local business. Citibank itself is a collection of eighty banks. Banks chose their own complex, rigid decision-making structures.

You either issue loans or write software. You cannot do both at once and equally well. Russia’s Sberbank has more programmers than Apple; so does JPMorgan Chase. Have they produced even one smartphone, or at least a mouse? No. Those people leave Sberbank and work perfectly well at Facebook. The problem lies elsewhere. A bank’s business model is lending. Apple’s business model is selling gadgets and software. Programmers will therefore always be secondary inside a bank, and the software will be worse. For software to become good, it has to be sold. The market will do the rest.

*Vityaz at the FinNext 2014 conference.*

**— How, then, did Privat manage to combine the two?**

— We decided to do so because PrivatBank was a private bank built by entrepreneurs who wanted to expand in domestic and foreign markets. Every decision was made immediately. Had I worked at a hypothetical UniCredit, I would have had to leave and open a startup to accomplish anything; otherwise, I would have been dragged through meetings, committees, and endless approvals. It was the leader’s decision. In most cases, decisions like that cannot be made at all because the decision-making system is complex and diffuse.

Besides, we did not merely write programs. We also conducted business. That is a unique combination that has not been reproduced in that form at any other bank in the world. Everywhere else, those functions are separated.

**— Really? In your view, is there no one else capable of combining the two?**

— Such people can be found, but the leader has to look for them and change the structure. As a rule, that never happens. It is easier to judge appearances. A hipster? Trousers rolled up? Then he must be an innovator. What comes next? He has never sold anything.

Here is an example. At the FinNext conference in 2014, the organizer decided without warning to stage a battle between me and Alfa-Bank. The bank’s representative spoke first. He was asked, “How do you make innovations?” Without blinking, the man replied: “We built an office and bought beanbags made by SUCH-AND-SUCH A BRAND. We have floor-to-ceiling windows. We lie on the beanbags and look out over Moscow…” He said this from a stage to an audience of two hundred people. Then he lasted another three years at the bank. They were all dismissed last year. During that time, they achieved nothing—and could not, because they were not engaged in business. They were all perfectly decent people; some had studied at MEPhI, others at Bauman Moscow State Technical University. They had simply never sold anything.

Haruki Murakami’s novels often use the term *salaryman*: a salaried employee without ambitions who merely carries out instructions. Armchair innovations do not exist. Innovations are born in a collision with the customer.

The issue is not some particular talent; talent can always be found. The decision-maker has to point at the map and say: “I want to be here.”

**— Nevertheless, banks place great emphasis on their own development work and fintech achievements. They are not alone.**

— What else can they emphasize? Deposits and loans are the same everywhere. Everyone behaves that way; it is reactive behavior. Everyone holds hackathons, so we will too. Everyone attends conferences, so we will too. This applies not only to banks. Retail and telecoms are the same.

**— How do you communicate with former Privat comrades—for example, Dmitry Dubilet?**

— We had little contact even inside the same company. Today, none.

**— How do you assess the monobank project?**

— From a business standpoint, A-plus. From the standpoint of technological innovation, there is none there—and there should not be. It is entrepreneurship in its purest form. It is very well executed. They knew how to do all of that at PrivatBank. They transplanted their competence into another bank, and we can all see the result.

**— What about presenting it under the banner of innovation?**

— You should do the same. *(Smiles.)* Innovations differ: technological, marketing, logistical, and so on. A retail product always needs a message. “Here is an instant card” was a relevant message eighteen years ago. “Credit card” was relevant ten years ago. Yesterday’s message was “cashback.” Tomorrow will require another.

**— So the fact that the younger Dubilet has caught the media wave is merely competent PR?**

— Why “merely”? Go and catch it yourself. I do not know how to catch media waves or conduct competent PR. If you are comparing us, do not. I am not a specialist in engraving metal, and my colleagues are not specialists in fog computing. They are consciously growing a community. If Facebook followers enjoy reading about engraving bank cards, then people find it interesting. You have to use everything that drives sales. That is the essence of business.

**Talk about kind and cruel bosses is 100 percent infantile. Companies operating under market conditions are always authoritarian because they must make decisions quickly.**

## 5. The market

**— How did you first begin dealing with global players such as Visa, Mastercard, and Western Union? Why would senior Western executives listen to an ordinary technical specialist from Donetsk?**

— Our first constructive contacts with Visa and Mastercard began when I was looking for a product that could issue cards instantly. Remember that, in those days, it took three or four weeks for a card to reach the customer. They did not have the technology, so Mastercard and we jointly gave birth to a global product: instant-issue cards. That was also when we realized that Mastercard was more agile than Visa.

**— Did the stereotypes begin to crack?**

— On both sides. We thought global companies were more intelligent than we were, while the global companies thought they would tell us what to do and how to do it. *(Smiles.)* Reality proved more complicated: in some areas they were smarter and more experienced; in others, we were faster and more entrepreneurial.

**— How did they perceive some unknown man from an unknown Ukraine?**

— Wait. This was not an unknown man from an unknown Ukraine. This was PrivatBank, already a member of Visa and Mastercard—a client that had passed certain procedures. What they found abnormal was that we constantly wanted something from them. *(Laughs.)*

There was one episode. In 2001, we launched one-time passwords by SMS, and Visa sent us a letter saying the practice had to be prohibited. The objection came from a security executive who was not even responsible for our region. The method supposedly violated standards, was not secure enough, and so on.

**— That view still exists in the market, broadly speaking.**

— Let it. People simply want to buy a lock that protects against everything: evil spirits, thieves, and fires—while they lie there doing nothing. *(Smiles.)*

**Whether you are Facebook, Tesla, or a kebab shop, you still have a bank account. That makes the bank a universal point through which you can enter anywhere.**

**— Roman Khimich, who appeared on our podcast, [believes](https://tech.liga.net/telecom/article/dayte-pin-vernite-kredit-6-faktov-o-telekom-shantaje-v-ukraine) that all these one-time passwords represent a major deterioration in security, and so on.**

— A deterioration? Measured in what? For a practitioner, the measure is fraud. One-time passwords reduced fraud; they did not increase it. They also allowed Privat24 and LiqPay to become open systems. Is that bad? It produced an explosion in growth for us.

**— But he says nothing is more reliable than a hardware token.**

— Of course not. No one argues in a cemetery either. I repeat: there are numbers, and there are market realities. The numbers show that our approach reduced fraud compared with a static login and password.

**— Compared with a static login and password, certainly. But compared with a physical electronic key?**

— How would you give every retail customer a flash drive in 2000? Even if you did, they would lose it within a week. It does not work. There are “experts,” and there are field scientists. One-time passwords reduced fraud. I did not introduce them for no reason: viruses were stealing static usernames and passwords with ease.

We are all clever now, but consider the skill level of users eighteen years ago. People were buying their first computers. Roman Khimich is therefore right as an “expert” and wrong as an entrepreneur. As a standalone instrument, a one-time password is insecure. As one component of comprehensive protection, it is a wonderful and flexible instrument.

**— How is this handled by European and American banks?**

— First, they have already implemented it. *(Smiles.)* Second, their payment infrastructure is entirely different. They did not need it as urgently as we did. American banks, for example, do not process utility bills or mobile-phone top-ups. They scarcely make external payments at all. Their entire fraud and anti-fraud focus is the credit card.

**— What about transfers between individuals?**

— Those too. A bank there handles loans and deposits. That is all.

**— Can the banking industry still do something unprecedented and global that surprises everyone? Automation is at an entirely different level today.**

— It can, but no longer inside a bank. Banks consume and invest in innovations; they are the last to create them. Visa was born inside Bank of America in 1959 and spun off into a separate corporation in 1969. But that is an exception. I described the subject in greater detail in my essay “Visa Is Killing Banks.”

**— So Visa is now becoming an IT provider?**

— It is not “becoming” one. It may or may not become one. That is an opportunity, not destiny. And Visa is not alone. Smartphone manufacturers, messengers, and other superpredators such as Google and Amazon also participate in the ecosystem.

**— Who can compete with them?**

— Alipay or WeChat, for example, if they find a way beyond China. But there are many reasons why they may not be allowed to accomplish anything.

We are promoting the idea of an algorithm marketplace. Today, 24,000 banks around the world—members of Visa and Mastercard—are courageously doing the same foolish work. They write online banks, mobile applications, and robots; they “develop technologies” for biometrics. All of it could be standardized.

We also approach clients directly. But the economy has a pyramid. Visa, Western Union, and SWIFT stand at the top. Banks form the second layer, businesses the third, and you and I—the customers—the fourth. That is the economy, but all of it passes through banks. Whether you are Facebook, Tesla, or a kebab shop, you still have a bank account. That makes the bank a universal point through which you can enter anywhere. Banks are the gateway through which the entire global economy can be accessed.

**— Which of your clients do you consider the most agile?**

— “Speed” is relative. What feels slow to me may feel fast to someone else. On average, startup clients move faster than large ones.

**— Do you have any contacts in telecoms?**

— We are working on it.

**— With Ukrainian operators too? They are investing heavily in new services.**

— With ours as well. Operators are local businesses, just like banks. They knew perfectly well about messengers. Their business model stood in the way because they earned money from SMS. British Vodafone had such a story. A team was building a messenger for Windows smartphones. It was miserable, but it more or less worked. Vodafone bought them for €60 million. It later emerged that Vodafone had bought three startups of that kind. But each time, at a certain stage, the director responsible for SMS appeared and said: “I earn you €15 billion a year. What free messages are you talking about?”

Banks and operators are both hostages to their business models. Today’s messengers are hostages to their own models too. They are turning into corporate swamps. Breakthroughs occur when you find a way beyond your model.

**— But operators are playing so actively at creating their own innovations. Are they seeing the light?**

— The key word is “playing.” And please do not take the word “innovation” in vain. *(Smiles.)* Their shareholders see the light only after spending hundreds of millions and obtaining no result. Telecoms need to understand that they require a vertical integrator. I am not sure the operators can agree among themselves. Messengers have already played the game for them. Operators were left with “connectivity”; communication went to the messengers. Google may come to the operators’ aid in their struggle with messengers. In any event, without a vertical integrator, they are doomed.

**— If so, what chance do operators have of reclaiming the communications market?**

— They always have a chance if they are willing to become investors. They could buy Facebook shares, for example.

**— So telecoms should accept that they are merely a “pipe”?**

— Everyone has to assess their place in the pyramid correctly, along with their ability to move through the hierarchy. How could Auchan become Amazon? Will it begin making films or enroll in programming courses? Today’s apex predators can no longer be caught. Companies must seek asymmetric opportunities to become digital apex predators. A digital superpredator is a global company that produces technologies and products based on them while enjoying direct access to customers. Such a company has a complete ecosystem of its own. Microsoft and Google, for example, are digital superpredators. Visa is not, because it lacks direct access to customers.

**— You position yourselves as a company capable of solving many of a client’s IT problems. But you are not a magic pill. How do you explain that to clients?**

— We are certainly not a magic pill. But one client in Central Asia hired us solely to stop the flood of purchasing inside the company. They have what I call a “shopping gene.” They had bought every piece of software in the world; half of it had not even been unwrapped. There was little benefit, but plenty of motion. Everyone tells a beautiful story, vendors arrive and make the bed soft… The shareholder contacted us to stop the purchases. The criteria for success vary greatly. Sometimes the first step is simply to stop and quit feeding the fire with banknotes.

**— Suppose a Ukrainian operator came to you and said: “Alexander, save us. We do not want to be a pipe.” What would you say?**

— I would ask: “Are you prepared to change your decision-making structure?” In 98 percent of cases, after that question they would return to running tenders and hackathons, while we would help their competitors develop. The first thing a large company has to do is want to change. Magical technologies come second in this story.

Operators possess value that extends beyond their towers. Every individual and legal entity has an account with an operator. That billing system could become the basis of a platform. But a platform needs not only customers—it also needs technology, which operators do not have.

**— Who are your key clients?**

— One of my strategic objectives is to have no key clients. We do not want to become the IT appendage of a large corporation. For me, the right “key client” is one from whom we can learn or through whom we can scale into other markets and industries.

**— Name three Ukrainian businesspeople you respect.**

— Everyone who does anything in our country deserves respect. The state has opposed entrepreneurs for 101 years. It strangles them and strangles them, yet cannot finish the job. That is a genuine miracle.

There are good entrepreneurs whose products I dislike. I nevertheless respect them as entrepreneurs. Ukraine has very few companies that produce core technologies. You can count them on one hand.

**— Is Ajax Systems, for example—a Ukrainian company then actively conquering the security market—considered a strong company?**

— It is. So are Petcube and GitLab. But those are primarily product companies. Only GitLab has the potential to become an ecosystem.

**— Can you name individuals after all?**

— If we reduce it to names, we risk becoming small-minded people in Eleanor Roosevelt’s classification. *(Smiles.)*

**— Yaroslav Azhnyuk or Vladyslav Chechotkin, for example?**

— Everyone has strengths and weaknesses. There are no straight roads to victory. I sincerely welcome every success achieved by our compatriots. To rephrase Pope Francis: who am I to judge them?

**— You say that combining business and technology is very rare. Which entrepreneurs at the global level know how to combine business and IT—whether in pure technology corporations such as Google and Facebook or in companies that successfully digitized themselves?**

— The combination is not rare, but it lives during a particular stage in a company’s life cycle. Every large company was once a startup. As it matures, however, the business component always wins. Emphasis on business or technology belongs to different stages of the corporate life cycle. First comes an idea, then technology, sales, and business.

Some companies can be reborn, but that is the exception rather than the rule.

Several years ago, I spoke with a senior Western Union executive. She asked what I would invest in. I answered Microsoft. She was very surprised. She had previously worked there, and Microsoft was then at the peak of pessimism. I explained that, although I disliked the company, as a gamer I could see the absolute dominance and growth of Windows as a home and gaming platform, together with the strongest sales system for enterprise software and so on. All of that would allow Microsoft to return to the top. We are now witnessing that magnificent revival.

At that moment, Microsoft was a compressed spring. It did not matter who ran it; the company contained a vast amount of technology. I can judge even from my own behavior: I spend as much time on a PC as on a MacBook. I play on the PC and work on the Mac.

**— Consoles exist for games.**

— A console is too hardcore for me. Microsoft is also the second-largest player in that market with Xbox. Consoles are a very large part of its graph. The company is looking intensely in that direction, and the strategy pays off. Few people I speak with have considered the PC as a game console, but that is precisely what it is. I bought every console, but understanding them and their services is difficult.

**— Gamers will disagree with you.**

— It is only my opinion. I am a novice who plays a great deal. *(Laughs.)* Consoles have their own gameplay, which does not suit everyone. I do not understand how to play *StarCraft* on one. Microsoft therefore did everything correctly: the PC as a gaming platform for everyone, Xbox for the hardcore.

**— Although Microsoft arrived late to the cloud.**

— Its enormous on-premises business and ambitions unsupported by competence slowed it down. Google managed to jump aboard an arriving train; Microsoft managed to leap onto one that was departing. Google and Amazon had a good head start and extracted everything they could from it. But Microsoft’s enterprise clients cannot simply get up and leave. The company sells them Word and Excel for the fifth time—now in the cloud. That is an ecosystem. If you have one and are not a complete idiot, you have eternal life. Microsoft had plenty of money. In other words, every factor was present for it to come to its senses and catch the market.

**— What stage is Tesla in?**

— Tesla is in the Big Bang stage, when technology and business move together. I do not know the company deeply enough, but what I saw at Tesla’s development office in October closely resembled the way we worked at PrivatBank: running forward and using every market and technological opportunity. *(Smiles.)* The cultures felt very similar.

**— What about Apple?**

— Apple is one of the ten digital superpredators. To paraphrase Dice: “Apple is on top of the world, ma.”

**— Yet the economic indicators of this company at the top of the world are deteriorating. Do you predict a repetition of Microsoft’s story—a spring being compressed again? What would drive the rebound?**

— Have they completely collapsed, then? You ask questions like a nervous housewife. Your problem is reactive behavior; you need to replace it with conscious behavior. *(Smiles.)* Apple is a complete ecosystem, an apex predator. There is no reason for it to fall apart. We are in an objective period of transition in technologies, hardware, and other components. The product, price, and quality need time to come back into harmony.

*Apple will be fine, Alexander Vityaz believes. Tim Cook is pleased to hear it.*

**The role of a CEO in a modern company that forms part of a large holding group is almost nominal. Provided you do not drink or smoke at work, everything will be fine.**

**— You said that messengers, although they took part of telecoms’ business, are themselves turning into corporate swamps. Which ones?**

— The money remains in telecoms for now. Messengers took communication—that is, customers—and capitalization from telecoms, not revenue.

**— Which are becoming corporate swamps?**

— Every one in which a team of business executives replaced the founding team: Skype, WhatsApp, Viber.

We communicate with managers at all those messengers. They are normal and intelligent, but they have MBAs. These messengers are already infrastructure. They are obliged to give users opportunities—that is the social debt owed by companies to which we users pay our attention and time. Yet the door remains closed.

A senior executive at one messenger said that the founders had left behind many technical problems and debts. Does that remind you of anything? *(Smiles.)* They had supposedly spent three years clearing it all up. Everyone is clever in hindsight. Why did you not found a company yourself, then, instead of taking a salaried job?

Conflict between founders and managers is natural. New companies are born under uncertainty. They search for themselves and evolve, accumulating large amounts of legal, managerial, process, and technological debt along the way.

A new company’s principal task is to survive. By definition, it cannot be perfect at the beginning or while it expands. Its task is to produce an optimal product that users begin buying—and to capture the market.

Formally, the messenger executive is correct when speaking about the founders’ technological debt. But these are objectively different stages of corporate development, and he should not speak about the founders without appropriate respect. *(Smiles.)* Dude, who created your job?

**— As people say of writing: editing is not the same as writing from scratch.**

— Correct.

**— What did you mean by a “closed door”? That they do not want to cooperate with you, or that they do not want to open globally?**

— Any business that becomes socially significant will be required to open itself to users.

That is how regulation works. European governments required banks to open. The same must happen to any telecom. Once you are socially significant—within a country or beyond it—you must have a glass door. That does not mean, “Come in and do anything you like.” But the door must exist. I can enter a restaurant. I cannot enter Vodafone or WhatsApp. Visit their websites and try to find an API.

**— You once said that you had offered advice to a Ukrainian telecom company. How did it use the advice?**

— It was not so much advice as proposed projects and cooperation. Everyone there has already been dismissed. The issue lies less in the people than in the industry itself.

**— When you say “everyone has already been dismissed,” do you mean Peter Chernyshov, for example—the former president of Kyivstar who left in July 2018?**

— Him too, although the statement applies to any company with “telecom” as a prefix. There is one simple fact: during all our years of communicating with Chernyshov, we accomplished nothing interesting together.

**— Everyone measures with their own ruler. In an interview with our publication, for example, he [said](https://tech.liga.net/telecom/interview/petr-chernyshov-mne-poka-v-ofis-voobsche-neohota-vozvraschatsya) that Kyivstar would remain fine for a year and a half after his departure.**

— It will remain fine for ten. That proves only that the CEO’s role in a modern company forming part of a large holding group is almost nominal. Provided you do not drink or smoke at work, everything will be fine. We knew a much more entrepreneurial Kyivstar, so I can compare before and after. We met the entrepreneurial Kyivstar in 1999, when it had 20,000 subscribers and we had fewer than 100,000 cards. During Kyivstar’s first five years, while it was young, we launched dozens of projects together—the world’s first electronic vouchers, SMS banking, and so on.

Chernyshov’s final interview was not especially clear because we never received the answer everyone expected to the most important question: “So what happened?”

Today, we expect more than preservation of what has been achieved. We expect revival and greater openness.

**A new company’s principal task is to survive. By definition, it cannot be perfect at the beginning or while it expands. Its task is to produce an optimal product that users begin buying—and to capture the market.**

## 6. The future

**— Ukrainian IT specialists inspire a great deal of discussion and pride. Do you believe Ukraine has an IT future?**

— Ukraine had an IT past, has an IT present, and will, of course, have an IT future.

**— What might that future look like, and how should people entering technical fields today adjust their course?**

— They should not adjust anything. They should study mathematics; it will never change. Educational institutions need to alter their course, but that is a separate and extensive discussion.

**— How can present and future technical specialists avoid losing themselves in the market?**

— They enter technical fields for some reason. Anyone who does not want to become lost will not. I have no formulas or life hacks.

**— Which areas of business and technology own the future, in your view?**

— The future is nonlinear. Do not search for simple mechanistic answers. In my version, the future belongs to knowledge, computation, and entertainment.

**— When someone launches a technology startup, what should they focus on, and what must they never do?**

— If someone is frying eggs, what should they focus on? The eggs.

**Today, the word “work” is a synonym for “life.” What will happen to those meanings when the two words cease to be synonymous?**

**— The automation debate has intensified recently. Robots, people say, will replace humans in the maximum possible range of activities, creating a global unemployment problem. Do you agree? What awaits traditional business over the next five years?**

— That is one contradiction piled on another. What is “human activity”—washing dishes or calculating prime numbers? What is work for?

In greatly simplified form, humanity has already experienced this. The robots of the future are the slaves of the past. But the laws of economics distribute every resource unevenly. Some people will therefore have many slaves—or more powerful ones—while most will have none. Yet those people will have no mechanistic work either. The questions of unemployment and bread therefore do not arise. No one wants to perform most of what is considered human work, and the food problem will clearly be solved once and for all. The theme of bread and circuses moves in a spiral. Read books about how to manage slaves and other ancient Greek and Roman sources.

The history of the ancient world shows how people addressed the unemployment of slave owners and the plebs, offering hints of possible answers for the future.

Moreover, the future is already here. Today, 50 percent of people in the world already live in that future, in a world without work: schoolchildren, retirees, and unemployed people receiving benefits. They have not degenerated or drunk themselves to death. On average, they are happier and better educated than everyone else. Observe them. They may be a projection of the society of the future.

**— But you yourself say some people will have many robot slaves while others have none. That creates a struggle for resources.**

— The old economic paradigm is built on scarcity. The new economy makes resources infinite—at least for basic human needs.

Statements such as “robots will take our jobs” are incorrect because the meaning of work will change. People will no longer need to work in order to exist. Today, the word “work” is a synonym for “life.” What will happen to those meanings when the two words cease to be synonymous? That is the question we need to answer—not whether something will wash our clothes and leave us with nothing to do at the weekend. You had a childhood, school, university, and holidays. Humanity is heading toward eternal student years. *(Smiles.)* How much fun we have during them certainly does not depend on robots.

**— Should we expect a change in meaning?**

— Why not? The search for meaning will remain a human activity for a long time. Until computer consciousness exists, humans will assign meaning to robots’ work. The future is here. There were already statistics showing eleven pensioners for every ten working people in Ukraine. More than 50 percent of people already live without working. The quality of that existence is another matter.

**— Exactly. But people as a whole still work.**

— Fewer than half, and the proportion is falling. Before the Industrial Revolution in England, children went to work from the age of eight. A child was considered merely a small human being; childhood did not exist as a distinct stage of life. That changed only about two hundred years ago. Three hundred years ago, everyone capable of moving worked.

Society will give birth to meanings and rules of some kind.

**— The economic model would seem to have to adapt.**

— The economy is adapting. Maslow’s hierarchy is changing. The question is how much free energy humanity consumes. Today, the coefficient is low. If it increases tenfold, that energy will provide for us completely, and no one will have to work to obtain it. An abundance of energy produces an abundance of food, clothing, and everything that can be replicated. Artificial sturgeon caviar is poorly made today. Tomorrow, technologies will solve the problem. Labor by the sweat of one’s brow is already ending.

**— Well, in a certain sense it has not ended yet.**

— Of course idol worshippers still exist, and the struggle against them has continued for two thousand years. Nevertheless, they lost long ago. You carry 1.5 to 2 percent Neanderthal within you, but Neanderthals no longer exist.

**— Following that logic, the very concept of a “profession” will disappear in the future.**

— Why? A profession as a specialization will remain. Science is infinite; we understand that. Art probably is too. There is plenty to do. Labor changes, yes. People do not disappear.

*Not only Vityaz but every Homo sapiens carries between 1 and 3 percent Neanderthal genes. Scientists believe that Homo sapiens inherited part of the immune system from this ancestor.*

**— In your view, will even intensely human professions such as psychology disappear?**

— That is the kind of question asked in *Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears*: “There will be nothing but television.” Psychologists will remain. Later, robots will appear there too. You play games, do you not? If you like a game, you play against the computer and enjoy it. It is a question of technological development.

The future is undetermined. There is no need to extrapolate it all the way to the end. The universe will end in twenty billion years. So what? That is the wrong way to think.

**— What is the right way?**

— Do not worry about it. Evolution has no goal. Go with the flow.

Most of the stars we see will live another five to ten billion years. In terms of the universe’s present age, new generations of stars will appear another five times. Can we think on a scale of fifty billion years? Or even one billion? No.

**— Sometimes looking five years ahead is already too much for us.**

— Five is reasonable. Beyond that, fortune-telling begins. We can think forward in years and backward in millennia. No further. If science and technology developed so dramatically in two hundred years, what might happen in a thousand? It is entirely impossible to predict.

**— Provided we do not blow one another to pieces.**

— We will not. Nuclear weapons today contain less energy than they did during the Cold War. Large cities may be blown apart, but collective farmers, lumberjacks, and miners will remain. Journalists, of course, will not survive. *(Laughs.)*

**— Of course not. We will all be reporting from the epicenter.** *(I laugh.)* **Let me ask something else. There was an era when the country scarcely had an IT industry; it was still embryonic. You caught that wave and developed with it. What about today? Modern children begin from an entirely different platform.**

— I cannot say what the answer is today. From the age of ten, I wanted a computer—not simply to possess one, but to write programs. Why? To solve problems and play games. A computer is a highly accessible way to make something at home, with guaranteed success if you do everything correctly.

For my children, by contrast, it is already part of the environment and everyday life, not a dream. That is entirely normal. I had an acute need to obtain information because I suffered from information hunger. I am not sure my contemporaries in the United States had that problem. The waiting list for *Robinson Crusoe* at the library was three months. Everyone wanted books, even people who barely read them. When you have abundance, you begin wanting something else.

**— What do they want, then?**

— You cannot assess it because you do not belong to that generation. They want whatever comes next.

**— Even innovations no longer have to be written from scratch in the old way—and you personally promote that shift. How are ideas born now?**

— Newton also stood on the shoulders of giants. Ideas are born; you simply cannot understand them because, from their perspective, you are a Neanderthal. For us, going to the circus and the philharmonic was ordinary. Who goes now? People still do, of course, but I mean on a mass scale. The same happened to cinema: it became niche. Access to information increased. Wonderful.

Anything can be created. Microbes living more than ten centimeters below the soil surface have scarcely been studied. There are tens or hundreds of thousands of species, and the connections among them are beyond comprehension. How many microbiologists are there in the world? Niches in which you can make a breakthrough are always opening. People want to make breakthroughs retrospectively, but that does not work.

Radio astronomy now produces so much data that there is discovery after discovery. Yet you do not examine it. You interview middle managers at international companies—and managers responsible for the CIS, at that. *(Alexander Vityaz is probably referring to the [interview with a senior Viber executive](https://biz.liga.net/all/it/interview/top-viber-my-ne-hranim-vashi-perepiski-v-otlichie-ot-telegram) that I conducted during the Odesa trip—Author.)* I am not trolling. Visit NASA’s website: there is an API and there is data, but you do not go there. Amateur astronomers accomplish a great deal because professional scientists can no longer keep up. Add viruses, and you obtain infinity multiplied by infinity.

**— Suppose that is simply outside my interest function.** *(I smile.)*

— You have to try. If you want to search for your interest function through interviews, then you need to conduct many more interviews.
