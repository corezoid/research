## Intelligence in the Bank

> To become an innovative company, hiring a brilliant programmer is not enough. You need to create an environment where every employee puts forward new ideas and managers strive to bring them to life. PrivatBank's experience shows how this works.

**Nina Mishchenko**  
*Forbes Ukraine, No. 10 (44), October 2014 · IT Leaders / Innovation*

Few people associate the banking system with concepts such as "innovation" or "high technology." Stability and reliability are discussed far more often. Judging by its main financial indicators, PrivatBank is a well-oiled machine. In 2013, it earned UAH 1.9 billion in net profit. Nearly 18 million Ukrainians use its cards - half of all active payment cards in the country. Raiffeisen Bank Aval is in second place, with 2.8 million cards. But PrivatBank knows how to do more than sell cards. One in every three clients accesses its services through the internet and mobile applications. In a single day, Privat24 users complete 325,000 online transactions. PrivatBank's mobile application is installed on one out of every two iPhones in Ukraine and one out of every four Android smartphones.

Today, these figures no longer seem as fantastical as they did back in 2000, when Chairman of the Management Board Alexander Dubilet made an important decision: PrivatBank would go online and become a digital company. Bill Gates's *Business @ the Speed of Thought* transformed the way he thought. "Wallet-size computers will provide identification and make electronic transactions possible," the Microsoft founder predicted in 1999. "Online payment systems will be introduced in most companies, and banks will maintain websites where consumers can pay their monthly bills."

Alexander Vityaz, then head of PrivatBank's plastic cards department, had given Dubilet the book. He bought several copies at the Moscow House of Books. "I devoured all 300 pages while traveling to Dnipropetrovsk. The book confirmed that we were thinking and moving in the right direction," Vityaz recalls. "Gates helped us develop a clear vision and inspired the other executives." Dubilet instructed every top manager to read the book and convened a special meeting just a few days later. Within a week, the price of the bestseller at the market near the office had increased fivefold.

Vityaz's name is almost unknown to the wider public. Yet he created most of PrivatBank's innovative developments. Without Dubilet's willingness to take risks and make decisions whose success few people believed in, however, the bank would hardly have been able to introduce new technologies so quickly. *Forbes* tells the IT story of PrivatBank.

> **MAN'S BEST FRIEND**  
> Alexander Vityaz agreed to be photographed only with his beloved dog.  
> *Photo: Nikita Zeletsky for Forbes Ukraine.*

Vityaz was born in Donetsk on July 20, 1969, the day Neil Armstrong landed on the Moon. A year later, when the famous American astronaut visited the USSR, Vityaz's father went out of his way to obtain an autographed photograph for his son. "Now I always celebrate the day together with NASA," Vityaz jokes. He dreamed of becoming an astrophysicist, but in the fifth grade he began programming on Elektronika MK-series programmable calculators.

His studies at the Faculty of Applied Mathematics of Donetsk Polytechnic Institute coincided with the collapse of the USSR. In his fourth year, Vityaz took a job as a loan officer; in the early 1990s, he began selling personal computers on a commodities exchange. "They cost more than an apartment back then," he says. "But at night I could explore the internet and write my first programs in an interesting programming language - LISP."

"Free your knowledge workers from routine and give them the opportunity to concentrate on work that requires real mental effort," Gates wrote in his bestseller. Monotonous work had always irritated Vityaz. Why spend time on something that can be automated? While working at the Donetsk branch of Inko Bank, he created a computer spreadsheet for calculating interest and built an electronic payment calendar.

> **"FREE YOUR KNOWLEDGE WORKERS FROM ROUTINE AND GIVE THEM THE OPPORTUNITY TO CONCENTRATE ON WORK THAT REQUIRES REAL MENTAL EFFORT."**

The talented programmer was noticed. As deputy director of the Donetsk branch, Vityaz launched the sale of travelers' checks and the first chip cards of the Inex Card system. But Inko was slowly going under. In 1996, the 27-year-old Vityaz moved to PrivatBank's Donetsk branch, which was among the first seven Ukrainian banks to begin working with the Visa payment system. Within a year, the branch had the strongest card sales outside Dnipropetrovsk.

How did it manage that? At the time, issuing a piece of "plastic" took three or four weeks because cards were manufactured at the head office. "We charged a client $100 for urgent card issuance - that was the tariff - sent a taxi to Dnipropetrovsk, and produced the card in a day or two. It sounds funny, but executives at competing banks sent their clients to me because they could not issue a card that quickly," Vityaz recalls.

He managed to buy one of the first embossers - devices that made it possible to issue plastic cards directly at a branch. Obtaining approval from the National Bank of Ukraine for this technical novelty was not easy. His success as a salesman introduced the young banker to PrivatBank's new chairman, Dubilet, who had come to Donetsk for a meeting with the mayor and stopped by the branch to see the record-breaking salesman. "I told him that we were the best here," Vityaz says with a smile.

At PrivatBank, Vityaz worked not only on card sales but also on automating the routine processes he disliked. In conversations with clients, he realized that most calls to a manager were about checking the balance on a card account. Pagers came to the rescue. One day, a seller of the devices, which received short text messages, walked into the branch. Vityaz bought several dozen and began offering a card together with a pager. Once a week, bank employees sent the account balance to the device. Around the same time, Vityaz had the idea of creating a dedicated call center to solve clients' problems.

To avoid confusion, the card number was linked to the pager number. The device's drawback, however, was the lack of a return channel. A mobile phone could solve that problem. By the late 1990s, three operators had received licenses for GSM communications, making it clear that mobile phones would become more affordable. This was also when the famous billboards appeared in Donetsk: "Don't be a fool - get a mobile."

Vityaz first described a system linking a bank card to a mobile phone, along with the possibility of communicating with clients by SMS, in a 1999 article for the bank's corporate magazine. After it appeared, Dubilet called Vityaz and invited him to work at the head office in Dnipropetrovsk. The chairman - as the CEO is often called at PrivatBank - was looking for a new head of the plastic cards department and was interested in the unusual proposal from the regional branch.

That is how Vityaz arrived in Dnipropetrovsk in May 2000. PrivatBank, meanwhile, received the country's first SMS banking service. "We tried to find banks that had the same service, but we could not," Vityaz says. "That is why we believe we were the first in the world to create SMS banking. It appeared in American banks only in the mid-2000s."

In addition to receiving information about card transactions, the bank's clients could top up a mobile phone and perform simple account operations using straightforward SMS commands.

Vityaz does not look like a typical bank employee. He is seen more often in jeans and a T-shirt than in a suit. One gets the impression that Vityaz divides people into two categories: those who understand what he is talking about, and those who do not. He puts journalists in the second category and often teases them. Vityaz finds long interviews and photo shoots boring. He came to the *Forbes* shoot with his Shiba Inu, Chudik, and agreed to be photographed only with the animal.

Today Vityaz is deputy chairman of the management board, with many victories behind him, along with patents and inventions carrying the label "first in the world." In the early 2000s, however, most top managers who had worked at the bank since its founding saw him as nothing more than a capable employee from a regional branch.

"In the case of SMS banking, the chairman made a strategic decision: not to sell a bank card unless the client provided a mobile phone number," Vityaz says. A number whose accuracy the bank trusted was called "green." A bank card could become proof of the client's identity, eliminating the need to carry a passport and an identification code. The user, meanwhile, was responsible for keeping the phone number secure and up to date.

It is difficult to say what convinced the other members of the management board that the bank was moving in the right direction: Gates's book or the chairman's decision.

## The IT Wins List

*PrivatBank's key innovative developments*

**1999-2000 - Mobile Banking.** The first bank in the world to offer clients SMS banking, allowing them to top up a mobile phone account at any time using simple SMS commands.

**2000 - Internet Banking.** Introduced for the first time in Ukraine; within a few years it became the round-the-clock Privat24. Today it is a portal where, in addition to account transactions, users can pay utility bills, buy insurance, purchase plane and train tickets, and book hotels and cinema tickets.

**2000 - Electronic Voucher.** The sale of mobile phone top-ups through POS terminals and ATMs.

**2000 - Dynamic SMS Passwords.** One-time passwords for internet banking, made possible by linking a card to a mobile phone number. They made it possible to eliminate fraudulent operations in internet banking, particularly in Privat24.

**2003 - P2P Transfers Between Cards.** PrivatBank conducted the world's first transfer of money between Visa cards.

**2003 - Instant-Issue Cards.** PrivatBank became the first in the world to begin mass issuance of instant MasterCard cards, activated immediately after the client signed the agreement.

**2005 - SiteHeart.** A customer chat with an intelligent robotic auto-responder that helps reduce support costs. It also allows payments from Visa and MasterCard cards to be accepted directly in the chat.

**2006 - Online P2P Transfers.** For the first time in the world, it became possible to sit at a computer and transfer money to Visa cardholders at any time knowing only the recipient's card number.

**2008 - LiqPay.** A new payment system that makes it possible to accept payments and transfer money worldwide using a mobile phone, the internet, and payment cards.

**2008 - Banking Application for iPhone.** iPay, the first banking application of its kind in the post-Soviet region.

**2009 - Electronic Change.** Its introduction at bank tills made it possible to reduce the enormous cost of circulating coins. Change could be credited to the client's mobile phone account or issued as an electronic voucher.

**2010 - Mobile Payment Terminal.** A compact device - a reader for a mobile phone - that makes it possible to accept Visa and MasterCard cards for payment.

**2011 - Electronic Cash Collection.** Immediate crediting of retail proceeds to an account on the day of collection, ten minutes after the cash collection bag was received.

**2012 - Contactless Cash Withdrawal.** Cash withdrawal from ATMs using a QR code read from the terminal screen by the Privat24 application on a smartphone.

**2013 - Contactless Android ATM.** The world's first ATM without a screen or keyboard, allowing users to withdraw cash with a mobile phone.

**2014 - Cloud Mobile NFC Wallet.** The Privat24 application for Android became the first in the world to make contactless payments from any bank account or card using a smartphone, without stickers or secure elements.

From 2000 onward, PrivatBank began changing rapidly. SMS banking was joined by online banking and the sale of electronic vouchers for mobile phone top-ups. "Many people did not believe such vouchers would be in demand," Vityaz says. "They said it would be fantastic if, after a year, we reached 1,000 sales a day. Just one week after launch, there were 3,000 transactions in a single day." It was a profitable business for the bank: mobile operators paid it 15 percent of every transaction. Today PrivatBank sells 1.5 million mobile phone top-ups a day.

Electronic payments in the early 2000s had a serious problem: hackers could steal ordinary passwords when a user logged in or conducted a transaction. For more complex authorization, the Visa and MasterCard payment systems used a special key fob with a one-time password generator. Vityaz once left his at home and could not make an urgent payment while traveling on business.

When he returned to Dnipropetrovsk, he found the key fob on his desk. Looking at his mobile phone, Vityaz realized that the two devices could be combined and that a one-time password could be generated by SMS. This gave the bank a practical form of two-step authentication. Today, even transactions carried out on a client's card by a manager at a branch are confirmed by the client with a one-time password.

PrivatBank develops its key software systems in-house. "The pace of change in purchased software is extremely slow," Dubilet explains. "We cannot afford to wait for months to make a few changes." Long implementation cycles and the high price of changes are often accompanied by opaque pricing - and, accordingly, corruption. "You are tied forever to the vendor's vision. The person who develops the tools will always be ahead of the person who buys them," Vityaz says.

> **"THE PERSON WHO DEVELOPS THE TOOLS WILL ALWAYS BE AHEAD OF THE PERSON WHO BUYS THEM."**

The speed of change is the main thing Dubilet has fought for and continues to fight for. What matters to him is not being late - and being first.

In 2002, MasterCard announced a forthcoming innovation in a banking magazine: instant-issue cards. PrivatBank's executives immediately called Brussels. After a consultant arrived, the programmers adapted the bank's internal software suite themselves, and PrivatBank became the first in the world to begin the mass issuance of cards that could be activated immediately after the client signed the agreement.

The top managers say they were given only a couple of months to launch the business: they feared that another large player would capture the market. That decision made it possible to reach roughly 18 million active bank cards in the country.

Amid this wave of transformation, Vityaz took charge of a special unit, the Electronic Business Center (EBC), which focused on startups. PrivatBank thus separated the development of new projects from the IT department responsible for technical support. "It is important to strike the right balance between innovation and support functions," Dubilet emphasizes. "If we lost payments or made transactions take longer, clients would not understand us."

> **THE EXHAUSTED EMPLOYEE**  
> This is what an EBC employee who worked harder than the others and did not manage to quit in time looks like.  
> *Photo: Nikita Zeletsky for Forbes Ukraine.*

The EBC continues to respond rapidly to new technologies. For example, only a couple of days after the launch of the new iPhone 6, Privat24 added the ability to confirm payment not only with passwords but also with fingerprints.

In the early 2000s, Vityaz read an article saying that Visa International planned to create a system for transferring money between cards in order to compete with the increasingly popular PayPal electronic payment system. "We began thinking about how to connect our own work to the announced product, while at the same time knocking on Visa's door," Vityaz recalls. "But it turned out that there were no rules for card-to-card transfers yet, and there was no product either. Visa created and tested P2P transfers together with us."

In 2003, PrivatBank completed the world's first money transfer between Visa cards. Three years later, it became the first to launch a P2P - person-to-person - transfer service that allowed a user to send money from a computer knowing only the recipient's card number.

In 2008, Vityaz transformed the technology into a new electronic payment service, LiqPay. To transfer money, a sender needed only the recipient's mobile phone number; the money would be sent to the owner's card linked to a LiqPay wallet. If no card was linked, the recipient could register on the website and transfer the funds to any Visa or MasterCard card.

"PrivatBank launches not only solutions that are already used successfully around the world, such as MasterCard PayPass in 2011, but also products that are genuinely new to banking as a whole," says Vera Platonova, General Manager of the MasterCard Europe representative office in Ukraine and Moldova. In her view, because leading banks are not afraid to experiment, the Ukrainian market is one of Europe's leaders in the development of innovative payments.

To enter the Electronic Business Center, one must go up to the sixth floor of PrivatBank's head office. The EBC looks more like a startup incubator than an ordinary bank office. A bicycle hangs from the ceiling, and a plastic skeleton appears on one of the chairs from time to time. "An employee who worked a great deal and did not manage to quit in time," the EBC staff joke.

At the center is a relaxation area: a book-filled shelving unit and three crates of fruit - apples, oranges, and bananas - delivered three times a day. Behind a column stand a pair of beanbag chairs where one can retreat to read or work on a laptop.

Vityaz makes sure that programmers and developers have more than a constant supply of coffee. The *Forbes* journalist arrived during a staff dietitian's lecture on healthy eating. In the evening, employees receive free, properly balanced meals so that they do not order pizza and cola. Vityaz studied the reasons productivity declines and, on the advice of an Austrian scientist, gives his programmers fish oil and calcium tablets.

Today, the EBC employs around 200 people, including about 60 in the software development laboratory. Other departments work on Privat24, the online chat service, and various e-commerce applications.

Vityaz, however, is far from a gentle or indulgent manager. His management methods are the stuff of legend: he readily parts company with people who cannot withstand the pace. Disgruntled employees once even created a website where they wrote, "He makes us read books and write reports about them." Those who "survive" gain invaluable experience.

Privat24 and an application that allows users to pay for fuel at filling stations from a smartphone were both developed in the EBC. Vityaz also created SiteHeart, a round-the-clock online chat service in which robots generate more than half the responses, making it possible to reduce the number of call center employees. Many of Vityaz's developments can be used beyond PrivatBank. They are standalone products that the bank sells as an IT company.

For its invention of a contactless ATM without a screen or keyboard, controlled by a smartphone, the PrivatBank team received first prize at FinovateSpring 2014, the world's largest forum for banking innovation.

Vityaz believes his latest development will surpass all the previous ones. He is creating a new cloud operating system, Middleware, that will make it possible to create and program any business process.

How does PrivatBank manage to introduce innovation so quickly? Vityaz is not the only reason. "Without Dubilet, the ideas of that thin young man from Donetsk in glasses would hardly have been implemented," says one former PrivatBank top manager. Without a chairman open to everything new, the bank probably could not have moved so quickly.

"To outpace its competitors, a bank must possess intelligence. This is less about the exceptional mental abilities of individual employees than about the bank's overall ability to make effective use of the best ideas generated by its people." Dubilet applied this idea from Gates's *Business @ the Speed of Thought* to create a spirit of innovation at PrivatBank.

He instilled in top managers the habit of reading books - and not only business books. "Terry Pratchett's *Discworld* series helped us understand that a cost center should be placed where it can operate at the lowest cost," says First Deputy Chairman Oleg Gorokhovsky, who was responsible for payment cards and payroll projects. "Fred Reichheld's *The Ultimate Question 2.0* prompted us to create a special department for working with dissatisfied clients."

After reading the books, top managers write essays that help them systematize the information and decide what can be applied. Every three months, PrivatBank's managers now meet as a strategic committee: they choose a book, discuss it, and publish each executive's essay on the internal portal. "This, too, is part of our corporate culture," Gorokhovsky says.

> Alexander Dubilet turned the bank into a powerful IT company.  
> *Photo: Vasilina Vrublevskaya for Forbes Ukraine.*

An important principle for Dubilet is that every idea has the right to survive at least as far as the experiment stage. Back in the mid-2000s, PrivatBank created a portal where any employee could propose an improvement to a business process. The employee's manager reviews the idea and, if it is valuable, may even award a bonus. If the proposal merits further development, the manager can send it to Dubilet with a single click.

That is how Gorokhovsky came up with a network of parcel lockers through which bank employees help clients order small goods online. The goods are delivered to a PrivatBank branch and stored in a secure parcel locker. The devices are popular in smaller towns, where residents are accustomed to receiving parcels by post and are still less comfortable using the internet.

Dubilet also made top managers learn the principles of programming and database work, and even required them to learn how to use mockups - models of user interfaces. "If a manager today does not deeply understand the IT sphere and what can be done with data, that manager cannot work effectively," the chairman explains.

The bank now has around a dozen software applications. One is PhotoKassa, which allows a user to photograph a bill and send it to the bank for payment. Another, *Car: Expense Tracking*, helps motorists calculate vehicle maintenance costs.

If an idea does not work, that is no disaster. "You need to understand the reasons and move on. People must not be punished for it, although it should not be forgotten," the chairman-innovator adds.

The latest innovation is a network of small EBCs in each business unit, able to correct errors quickly or add individual services to Privat24. "We have obtained a tool for rapid modification of the system. I can go to my deputy responsible for IT and change something or fix an error today, rather than two weeks from now," Gorokhovsky explains.

"A corporate culture like this gives employees a daily sense of involvement and of the importance of their role in the success of the business," says Vladimir Kolomoyets, a client partner at Pedersen & Partners Executive Search. He believes that even if an employee continues to feel like a cog in a machine, the employee now understands that without that cog, the system will no longer work as it did before. According to the headhunter, this approach is highly motivating, making it extremely difficult to lure managers away to other companies.

Marina Kvashnina worked at PrivatBank for around three years. In November 2013, she became head of business development at Nikolay Lagun's Delta Bank, where her responsibilities included the development of new products, the payments business, and processing. On arriving at Delta, she began by separating technical support from the development of new projects. Delta Bank has since updated the payments component of its internet banking system and launched a P2P application with transfers by phone number.

"PrivatBank is a flagship example of how sound, systematic investment in IT and the development of a corporate culture of innovation can radically change the principles by which an entire country's banking business operates," Kvashnina says. In her view, the great challenge for every leading bank is to find its own distinctive path rather than copying the solutions of the market leader "speeding down the autobahn."

"The fact that PrivatBank is ahead of many banks not only in Ukraine but also in Europe when it comes to innovation is highly motivating for the other market participants," says Tamás Hák-Kovács, Chairman of the Management Board of OTP Bank. He says that any bank deciding to introduce something new now understands that it has to be a very good solution because PrivatBank has set the bar so high.

"They do everything in-house, which is why they are fast. And the cost of developing and implementing these solutions is spread across 15 million people, not 500,000 clients. Competing is extremely difficult," Hák-Kovács says.

Can a specialist as talented as Vityaz be lured away? The head of the EBC believes not. Dubilet created an environment Vityaz is unlikely to find elsewhere: freedom of action, investment, and the ability to introduce innovations at PrivatBank. "Headhunters stopped calling me a long time ago. I am 100 percent not their kind of candidate," Vityaz says dismissively.
