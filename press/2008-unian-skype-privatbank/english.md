Skype, the advanced communications platform, was already widely used by Ukraine’s more IT-savvy public. Although the company had existed for five years, it was only now making its official entry into Ukraine, with PrivatBank as its partner.

The joint project was presented in Kyiv the previous day at a press party marking the agreement between Skype and PrivatBank. Before the event began, journalists carefully noted the names, surnames, and positions of the principal speakers: Alexander Vityaz, Head of PrivatBank’s Electronic Business Centre; Enrico Noseda, Skype’s Director of Business Development in Europe; and Alexander Dubilet, Chairman of PrivatBank’s Management Board.

The opening maintained an air of intrigue. The presentation began without the bank’s chairman, which naturally seemed rather strange. Noseda described the achievements of Skype—the internet communications platform—in figures. To avoid overburdening the reader, we will mention only a few: the Skype download system had recorded more than one billion requests; more than 370 million users had chosen its services; and Skype users collectively spoke for one billion minutes every week, 25% of them in video mode. These and other figures allowed Noseda to claim that Skype was “becoming the world’s most popular communications platform.” He also demonstrated the Skypephone, although its use in Ukraine still remained a prospect for the future.

*From left: Alexander Vityaz, Head of PrivatBank’s Electronic Business Centre; Enrico Noseda, Skype’s Director of Business Development in Europe, with an interpreter; and Alexander Dubilet, Chairman of PrivatBank’s Management Board.*

A practical and accessible way to communicate via Skype in Ukraine was demonstrated with Dubilet’s direct participation. He began addressing those present through Skype and then “teleported” into the hall where the presentation was taking place. In his remarks, alongside Skype’s other advantages, he drew attention to the emotional dimension of this form of communication.

“When you call someone, they do not always know what you are doing—whether you are reading a book or watching television. This has led to a certain devaluation of telephone conversations. But when we use Skype and look the other person in the eye, that emotional dimension makes the conversation more effective,” Dubilet said. He added that this form of communication should be useful both in people’s everyday lives and in business. A family example was demonstrated in the hall, where two brothers used a Skype video call to speak with their parents, who were working in Portugal.

Through the cooperation between PrivatBank and Skype, Ukrainian Skype users would gain easier access to a broad range of communications options: free Skype-to-Skype voice calls, video calls to other Skype users, free instant messaging, and affordable international calls to mobile and landline phones.

The agreement enabled the companies to create a co-branded version of the Skype software client, available for download at [skype.privatbank.ua](http://skype.privatbank.ua). The partnership programme also gave Ukrainian users, including businesses, straightforward access to online payments and Skype voucher top-ups through thousands of self-service kiosks, more than 5,750 ATMs, and nearly 3,000 branches across Ukraine. Calls to other Skype users were always free, while Skype vouchers were required to make low-cost calls to mobile and landline phones through the service.

Alexander Vityaz also said that the bank planned to launch several more joint projects with Skype in early 2009. These included issuing special plastic cards and integrating Skype with PEOPLEnet.

Surprisingly, PrivatBank did not consider the financial component of the project its main priority. The bank’s immediate objective was to reach one million Skype users in Ukraine, even though it expected 95% of the services to be provided free of charge.

“Today, the bank has more than 13.5 million individual customers and over 300,000 corporate customers. We would like Skype to become the principal means of communication between customers and the bank. I would like to stress that this communication is entirely free and does not require money in the account. Funds are needed only if you want to call a landline or mobile phone in America or Russia. If you use Skype numbers, no funds are required,” Dubilet said.

Reliable connectivity becomes even more important when time and distance are no longer barriers to communication. At the time, users needed only to download Skype from PrivatBank’s website and follow the simple prompts. They could then find friends and partners around the world and hold video conferences, negotiations, and meetings with the people they needed.

**P.S.** When I arrived home, I decided to test in practice what had been discussed at the presentation. Using Skype and PEOPLEnet, I was indeed able to speak by video with relatives living in Western Europe. It took me about 40 minutes to install the Skype software and the necessary equipment and establish the connection. It should be noted that most of the steps were completed by trial and error, while my two-year-old son took a direct and active part in the entire setup process.
