# WHY DO YOU NEED THIS BOOK?

*“A change in perspective is worth 80 IQ points.”*  
*Alan Kay, Turing Award laureate*

We all know how to swear. Most of us do it rarely, some like to season their speech with it, and there are those who speak almost exclusively in profanity — which, as a rule, is not very easy on the ear. But there are also virtuosos in whose mouths profanity turns into poetry. Let us read Les Podervianskyi, **a Ukrainian absurdist playwright**:

You will tell a child about your work in simple words, a client — in a businesslike tone, and a colleague — in professional terms. The language is the same, but the registers differ: the words, the intonation, the degree of formality and expressiveness all change.

From here on, by register I will mean the set of concepts and ways of reasoning that a person or a company is able to use. To develop this register is to master new concepts, notice connections, and ask questions that were previously beyond your reach.

A company’s register includes its management models, accumulated experience, ways of making decisions, and the registers of its employees, partners, and clients that are available to it. It is formed in the interaction of people and software and becomes embedded in knowledge, rules, and processes.

When we communicate, our registers **automatically adjust to the register of the other person** in order to achieve maximum mutual understanding. We pay for this with asymmetry: a schoolkid cannot raise their register to the level of a PhD — they simply lack the necessary conceptual apparatus. The PhD, on the other hand, can easily lower their register to the schoolkid’s level without losing the meaning of the message.

A range of registers and the ability to fine-tune to your listener are a mark of linguistic flexibility and intelligence.

The constant broadening and deepening of the register is the principal product of civilization. The regular development of the register is done first and foremost by professional researchers and companies. Working adults, once out of school or university, for the most part remain outside these processes and fall back to the rearguard of evolution.

But as early as the nineteenth century, even polymaths — universal scholars — were finding it ever harder to keep up with the growth of knowledge. Scientific literature was growing exponentially: by Derek de Solla Price’s estimate, its volume doubled roughly every 10–15 years. Reaching the frontier of the unknown in even a single direction required ever more specialized training. Less and less time remained for mastering parallel disciplines at a comparable level.

Meanwhile, new complex systems are entering our everyday lives ever faster. We had decades to get used to email, years for smartphones — and AI demands that we raise our register right now.

And here we stumble against the limit of our own range: our conceptual apparatus is precisely the bottleneck in mastering complex systems.

Communication with AI works in exactly the same way as between people of different registers, except that the widest register always belongs to the AI, and we are cast as the eternal schoolkid, undergraduate, grad student, and so on.

AI instantly understands who it is talking to and adjusts to the other person’s register. This adjustment masks the gap: we are physically incapable of raising our register to the level of AI, simply because AI has read all the books (there are 100 million+ of them), while any given person will manage hundreds, a few thousand at most.

A smart answer from AI does not mean you have become smarter. We can learn to get excellent answers and still fail to notice that the question itself should have been asked differently. This robs us of the incentive to grow — the need to raise our own register so as to formulate and understand at a higher level.

So-called prompt engineering tries to “solve” the problem, but that is like taking an exam with a cheat sheet — cheating for the grade: you “get” the result, but nothing changes in your system of knowledge.

However, accumulating knowledge and experience alone is no longer enough to keep up with the growth of complexity.

Therefore, to raise our register, we need to learn to manage it: to consciously change perspective and move to the meta-level — to understand how our knowledge and our ways of thinking are built.

According to Alan Kay, **a pioneer of object-oriented programming**, a change in perspective is worth \+80 IQ points: from an average person (100) to a genius (180).

It is the search for a point of view from which the problem stops being a problem and becomes self-evident.

All that “remains” for us is to learn to switch our register to genius level (and higher, if you can afford it).

Raymond Cattell, **a British-American psychologist**, divided intelligence into crystallized — accumulated knowledge and experience — and fluid — the ability to make sense of the new. **Knowledge is a map of the terrain; the ability to learn is the skill of finding your way and drawing maps.**

AI will help us acquire the knowledge we need, while fluid intelligence we will develop by learning to describe the world as actor graphs.

In this book we will first take a fresh look at our technological environment and the vectors of its development, and then study the algorithms for describing, understanding, and managing complex systems — the tools for developing personal and corporate registers. We will treat people, software, and companies as actors, and complex systems as graphs of their interactions. Hence the title — *Codex of Actors*.

If you’ve made it through this introduction, go ahead and buy the book — you already have an idea of what lies ahead. If you haven’t, you still come out ahead: at the very least you’ve learned a lot of new words, saved some money, and know what to give your boss.
